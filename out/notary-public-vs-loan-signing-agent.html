Notary Public vs. Loan Signing Agent: What Is the Difference?

Disclosure: This page includes affiliate links. If you buy or sign up through them, The Bearded Notary may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.

The short version is simple: a loan signing agent is a notary public, but with a narrower and more specialized role. If you are trying to choose your path, the real question is not which one is better. It is which one fits where you are right now.

If you are still working toward your commission, start with the California notary guide or the California notary cost breakdown. This page is for understanding what comes after that commission and how the earning paths split.

Quick Answer

Notary public: the base commission that lets you perform authorized notarial acts.

Loan signing agent: a notary who also handles mortgage closing appointments.

Best first step: become a notary first, then decide if signing work fits your goals.

The Simple Difference

A notary public is commissioned by the state to verify identity, witness signatures, and complete notarial certificates correctly. That work can include powers of attorney, affidavits, deeds, permission slips, and countless other routine documents.

A loan signing agent is a notary public who focuses on real-estate and mortgage packages. They travel to borrowers, guide the signing flow of large document sets, return completed packages, and often print and scan documents as part of the job.

The relationship is one-way: every loan signing agent must already be a notary, but a general notary does not have to do loan signings.

What a General Notary Usually Does

  • Meet signers for everyday documents that need notarization
  • Verify identity and willingness
  • Complete certificates accurately and keep journal records where required
  • Travel to homes, hospitals, offices, care facilities, and public meeting spots
  • Build business through local search, referrals, and repeat clients

This side of the business is often a better starting point because it teaches the fundamentals without forcing you into large document packages right away. It also pairs naturally with local SEO, repeat community work, and the kinds of appointments that come from a strong online visibility strategy.

What a Loan Signing Agent Usually Does

  • Print large mixed-size loan packages accurately
  • Meet borrowers for refinance, purchase, seller, and HELOC signings
  • Keep the appointment moving while staying within the limits of notary law
  • Watch for signature, date, and initial errors before shipping documents back
  • Handle scan-backs, drop-offs, scheduling windows, and service expectations from title or signing companies

That is why the loan signing path usually requires more gear and a stronger workflow. If you want to go in that direction, the printer guide and scanner guide become much more important.

Loan signing work: higher-paying appointments, but more pressure, more gear, and more operational detail.

Best long-term play for many people: build a strong notary foundation, then add signing work if the workflow suits you.

Which One Should You Start With?

Start with general notary work if:

  • You are newly commissioned and still building confidence
  • You want a simpler entry point
  • You would rather learn local marketing and appointment flow first
  • You do not want to buy bigger equipment right away

Add loan signing work if:

  • You want higher-ticket appointments
  • You are comfortable with deadlines and large document packages
  • You are willing to invest in better systems and equipment
  • You want to build toward a stronger income ceiling

For most people, the healthiest answer is not either-or forever. It is more like a progression: commission first, fundamentals second, specialization third.

If you decide to move into signings, structured training can shorten the learning curve. Programs like Loan Signing System are one option to research once you know you want the signing-agent path.

A Good First-Year Path

  1. Get commissioned and learn your state rules well.
  2. Start taking general notary work and tighten your workflow.
  3. Build visibility with listings, reviews, and repeat local relationships.
  4. Upgrade your gear only when the work justifies it.
  5. Move into loan signings once you want the larger appointments and can support them cleanly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a notary public and a loan signing agent?

A loan signing agent is a notary public who specializes in mortgage and real-estate closing appointments. All loan signing agents are notaries, but not all notaries are loan signing agents.

Do I need to become a notary before becoming a loan signing agent?

Yes. You must first become a commissioned notary public before you can work as a loan signing agent.

Do loan signing agents make more than regular notaries?

In many cases, yes. Loan signing appointments usually pay more than general notarizations because the work is more specialized and operationally demanding.

Can I start as a general notary and become a loan signing agent later?

Yes. That is a very common path. Many people start by learning general notary work, building confidence, and then adding loan signing services once they are ready.

Is a loan signing agent better than being a notary public?

Not automatically. It is usually better for people who want higher-ticket work and are willing to handle printers, scan-backs, deadlines, and more complex appointments. A general notary path can still be a great fit depending on your goals.
